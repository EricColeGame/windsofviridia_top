## The Three Layers of Winds of Viridia Progression

If the game follows the structure its reveal implies, your advancement will happen on three separate tracks at once. Keeping them straight is the single biggest thing you can do to avoid frustration.

| Layer | What you're really collecting | How to advance it | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geography | Routes, shortcuts, vantage points | Follow paths to their ends, then branch at junctions | You keep re-walking the same corridor |
| Companionship | Trust and utility from your mothbug | Use it constantly and watch how it reacts | You forget it exists mid-explore |
| Knowledge | Patterns, landmarks, and secret logic | Test suspicions and revisit changed areas | You're stuck waiting for a quest marker |

Geography is the most visible layer. Every path you fully walk becomes a mental tool you can reuse later, and every shortcut you open permanently shortens future trips. Companionship is the least visible, but in games built around a single partner creature, that relationship often doubles as a soft key — the companion notices things, reaches places, or reacts to hazards you can't perceive alone. Knowledge is the layer that separates a smooth playthrough from a stalled one, because it's the only layer the game can't hand you.

Treat all three as progression currencies and you'll rarely feel lost.

## How Interlinking Paths Change Your Route

"Interlinking paths" is the phrase that should shape your whole approach. It implies a world built as a network rather than a corridor — loops that reconnect, shortcuts that pay off later, and detours that look pointless until they aren't.

| Path type | What it feels like in the moment | Best practice |
|---|---|---|
| Mainline trail | Wide, obvious, forward-leaning | Push to the next junction, then branch before continuing |
| Loop | Curves away, then returns near where you started | Finish it — loops tend to hide the best secrets |
| Shortcut | Opens a faster route you'll use repeatedly | Note it mentally and confirm where it lands |
| Dead-end detour | Feels like a mistake | Check walls, ceilings, and ledges before turning back |
| Vertical route | Climb up or drop down | Look up before committing to a descent |

The practical takeaway: when you hit a junction, don't save it for later. Clear one branch fully, return, then take the other. In interlinked worlds, unfinished branches pile up fast, and a stack of half-explored routes is the number one cause of that vague "what was I doing?" feeling.

Backtracking isn't a failure state in this design — it's the mechanic. Areas you've already visited often change meaning once you've gained a companion ability or noticed a pattern you missed the first time. Build one deliberate return trip into every play session.

## Companion Habits That Pay Off

The mothbug is the most distinctive element of the reveal, and it's almost certainly the most underused tool in most players' hands. Build these habits early.

- **Check in constantly.** Pause near new scenery and let the companion react. Behavior changes are free information.
- **Use it as a marker.** If the mothbug lingers somewhere, treat that spot as a note on your mental map.
- **Test it near hazards and heights.** Companions in this style of game usually solve specific traversal problems.
- **Don't sprint past its prompts.** If the game slows down or the companion circles something, stop.
- **Revisit old areas with new abilities.** A companion that gains reach or perception retroactively unlocks earlier dead ends.

None of this requires a menu. It's observation, and observation is the cheapest progression currency you have.
